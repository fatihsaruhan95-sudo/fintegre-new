When several buyers offer to purchase the same device, the highest price is not always the best result. A more useful comparison is expected net recovery: what the program can expect to receive once conditions, costs and timing are taken into account.

What a headline price leaves out

A buyer’s headline price usually assumes a specific condition, a delivery window and the buyer’s own checks. If any of these change, the amount settled can differ from the offer. Two offers with the same headline price can lead to different results.

Offers can also differ in what they include. One buyer may collect devices from the store, while another expects the program to arrange delivery. One may pay soon after receipt, another on a longer schedule.

For the program, the difference matters twice. It affects the margin on each device, and if credit was set from the headline price, the program may carry a gap between the credit given and the amount recovered.

Expected net recovery

Expected net recovery is an estimate of what a program will recover from a buyer for a device, after likely adjustments and costs. It brings together:

  • The offer price for the assessed condition.
  • How likely the buyer’s check is to confirm that condition.
  • Logistics and handling costs to reach that buyer.
  • Payment terms and timing.
  • How long the offer is valid compared with the expected handover date.

The estimate does not need to be exact to be useful. It needs to be consistent, so that offers are compared on the same basis, and it needs to be updated as completed transactions show how each buyer actually settles.

Factors to compare

  • Condition terms: how the buyer grades devices and how disputes are handled.
  • Re-grading history: how often the buyer’s assessment has matched the original assessment in completed transactions.
  • Logistics: where the device must be sent, who pays and how long it takes.
  • Payment terms: when and how the buyer settles.
  • Offer validity: how long the price holds and what happens if the device arrives later.
  • Capacity: whether the buyer can take the expected volume for that model.

Not every factor carries the same weight in every program. A program that needs settlement quickly may weigh payment terms more heavily; a program with limited logistics may prefer buyers who collect locally.

Using completed transactions

The best evidence for these factors is the program’s own completed transactions. Over time, they show which buyers settle close to their offers and which adjust more often. Where there is little history, treat estimates with care and review them as records build up.

Record the reason for every settlement that differs from its offer. Without the reason, a difference cannot be traced to the buyer, the original assessment or the timing.

Look at differences by model and condition as well as by buyer. A buyer may settle closely for recent phones but adjust more often for older devices, and the comparison should reflect that.

Making the comparison practical

Staff at the counter should not have to weigh these factors themselves. The comparison can run in the background, so the customer sees one clear credit value and the device is routed to the eligible offer with the strongest expected net recovery under the program’s rules. Review those rules regularly with commercial and operations teams.

Keep a record of which offer was selected for each device and why. This supports reviews with buyers and makes routing decisions easy to explain to commercial teams.

Sharing results with buyers

Comparing offers is not only an internal exercise. Sharing settlement records with buyers helps resolve recurring differences, for example a grading standard that is applied differently. Clear, shared records support a fair conversation about condition terms and prices.
