Designing subscription and device rental programs

Subscription and device rental programs give customers access to devices through recurring or term-based payments. This guide explains how the two models differ and what to define before launch.

Subscription and device rental programs give customers access to devices through recurring or term-based payments. On a product page they can look alike, but the commercial model behind each is different. Good design starts with being precise about what the customer pays for and what happens at the end.

Subscription programs

Subscription programs offer device access through recurring payments, with defined options for renewal, upgrade or return under the subscription agreement. The agreement sets the payment, the term and the options at each renewal point.

Whether an upgrade is available, when and under which conditions is part of the agreement. It should not be presented as a given.

Design the renewal points first: when they occur, what the customer can choose at each one and what each choice means for payments and the device.

State who owns the device during the subscription. If ownership does not transfer to the customer, say so plainly.

Device rental programs

Device rental programs offer devices for an agreed rental term, with clear payment, usage and end-of-term return conditions. Rental is defined by its term: the agreement sets the payment, how the device may be used and the condition expected when it comes back.

Rental can suit customers who need a device for a known period, or businesses that need devices for a project. Because the term is fixed, the program can plan when devices are due back.

Usage conditions matter more in rental, because the same device may be issued again. Set out what normal use looks like and how wear is assessed at return.

What to define clearly

  • Who owns the device during the agreement.
  • The payment amount, frequency and term.
  • What is included and what is not. Do not assume that insurance, repairs or replacements are part of the program unless they are explicitly offered.
  • Cancellation terms and any charges.
  • Upgrade options, if any, and their conditions.
  • The condition expected at return and how it is assessed.
  • What happens if a device is not returned.

Each of these points should be answered in the agreement and reflected in how the program is described. If an item is optional or depends on the market, say so.

Avoiding common misunderstandings

Customers may assume that a subscription works like a phone contract, that they will own the device at the end, or that they can cancel or upgrade at any time. None of these should be implied unless the program actually offers them. Use the same terms in marketing, at the point of sale and in the agreement.

Test the wording with people who have not seen the program before. If they describe it back differently, the description needs work.

Choosing between the models

Subscription suits programs that want an ongoing relationship with defined options to renew, upgrade or return. Rental suits a defined need with a clear end date. If you offer both, keep their names and terms separate so customers can tell them apart.

Planning for returned devices

Both models end with devices coming back. Returned devices need condition assessment and a route to their next use: another term, refurbishment or sale to a qualified buyer. Planning this at the start helps set realistic terms, because the expected value at return is part of the program’s economics.

Keep a record for each device across the agreement: when it was issued, its condition at issue and its condition at return. This supports fair condition checks at return and helps estimate values for future terms.

Agree in advance who is responsible for assessment, storage and any onward sale, and how the proceeds are accounted for in the program.
